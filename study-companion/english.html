Study Companion

A general learning support resource for nursing students, based on the Nursing Faculties Guide and its listed courses. Its purpose is to build a foundation for understanding that supports study success and helps students use knowledge meaningfully in later learning, professional practice, and service to people.

Build understanding first. Then return to lectures, your course instructor, and approved materials for detailed course requirements and assessment.
Pass marks and grading: each course is graded out of 100. The minimum pass mark is 60% in core nursing and supporting medical courses, and 50% in general courses. Excellent: 85–100 · Very Good: 75–<85 · Good: 65–<75 · Pass: from the applicable pass mark to <65.

English in nursing is not only vocabulary. It supports accurate questions, clarification, understanding instructions, reading scientific terms and transferring information safely.

Language foundations

Nouns & pronouns

patient, nurse, medication, wound, he, she, it.

Verbs

breathe, take, check, measure, turn, report, record.

Adjectives

severe, mild, swollen, clean, painful, warm.

Questions

Build short, focused questions to obtain specific information.

Position words

in, on, under, above, below, between.

Numbers & time

Essential for vital signs, doses, measurements and documentation.

Communication accuracy

Could you describe the pain?Ask for description, not only a yes/no answer.
Where exactly do you feel it?Clarify location.
When did it start?Clarify timing.
Do you mean that ...?Paraphrase to check meaning.
Can you show me what you understood?Check understanding after instructions.
Plausible situation

An unclear patient description

Rather than guessing, ask for clarification and repeat the meaning back to confirm it.

Why it matters: clarification and checking understanding reduce communication errors.

Anatomy explains body structure and location. Physiology explains how those structures work. Nursing becomes easier when students connect location, structure, function and what changes when function is disturbed.
Chemical
Cells
Tissues
Organs
Systems
Organism

Foundational ideas

Structure ↔ Function

Structure supports function.

Homeostasis

The body keeps internal conditions within workable ranges.

Feedback

Change is detected and regulatory responses follow.

Anatomical language

Directions, planes, regions and cavities make location precise.

Cells & tissues

Specialised cells form tissues that cooperate in organs.

Systems interact

No body system works in isolation.

Foundation lessons

Lesson 1

Anatomical language

Anatomical position, directions, planes, regions and cavities.

Lesson 2

Homeostasis

Receptors, control systems and responses help maintain suitable internal conditions.

Lesson 3

Chemistry of life

Water, ions, acids, bases and organic molecules support cellular reactions.

Lesson 4

The cell

Membranes regulate exchange; nuclei and organelles perform specialised roles.

Lesson 5

Membrane transport

Gradients, carriers and energy-dependent transport help explain fluids and cellular balance.

Lesson 6

Tissue families

Epithelial, connective, muscle and nervous tissues.

Lesson 7

Inflammation & repair

Tissue damage triggers protective and repair responses.

Lesson 8

Nervous system

Central and peripheral systems receive, process and coordinate responses.

Lesson 9

Heart & vessels

Cardiac pumping and vascular function maintain circulation.

Lesson 10

Blood

Plasma, red cells, white cells and platelets have distinct roles.

Lesson 11

Endocrine system

Hormones regulate many body functions.

Lesson 12

Lymphatic system

Fluid return and immune defence.

Laboratory thinking

When viewing a model, slide or image, ask: Where is it? How do I identify it? How does structure support function?

Example

Dizziness on standing

Connect circulation, blood pressure regulation and autonomic control.

Example

Redness and swelling near a wound

Connect visible signs with tissue inflammation and repair.

Example

Weakness in one limb

Think about nervous pathways and organised observation.

Example

Blood-count components

Understand why red cells, white cells and platelets have different meanings.

Health and illness are influenced by social conditions as well as biological factors. Family, education, income, work, housing, culture and access to care can shape health behaviour and outcomes.

Patterns of disease

Health risks are not distributed equally.

Social inequalities

Resources and access can change outcomes.

Illness experience

The patient's experience may differ from the biomedical label.

Culture

Beliefs can influence communication, food, privacy and help-seeking.

Sick role

Societies have expectations around illness and recovery.

Health systems

Organisation and service design shape the patient journey.

Example

A patient misses follow-up

Consider transport, cost, understanding, family support or work before labelling the patient “non-compliant.”

Example

Advice that cannot be followed

A technically correct plan may fail if it ignores the patient's real circumstances.

Nursing is not a list of procedures. Theory helps students ask: Who is the person receiving care? What is health? How does environment matter? What is the nurse's role?

Person

A whole person with needs, values, relationships and culture.

Health

More than absence of disease; includes function, adaptation and independence.

Environment

Physical, social and cultural surroundings that influence health.

Nursing

Observation, assistance, education, protection, relationship-building and coordination.

Selected perspectives

Nightingale

Environment matters

Cleanliness, ventilation, light, quiet and surroundings can influence recovery.

Henderson

Support independence

Assist with what the person would do independently if able.

Orem

Self-care

Assess what the person can do and where nursing support is needed.

Peplau

Therapeutic relationship

Communication and trust are part of professional nursing care.

Roy

Adaptation

Consider physical, psychological and social adaptation.

Leininger

Culture and care

Respect meaningful cultural beliefs and practices whenever safe and possible.

This is the course-description distribution; follow the officially announced current-semester distribution.
Biochemistry explains body processes at the molecular level: water and pH, carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, enzymes, vitamins, minerals, nucleic acids and metabolism.

Water & pH

Water supports reactions and pH affects protein and cell function.

Carbohydrates

Glucose is a central fuel molecule.

Lipids

Store energy, form membranes and travel through lipoproteins.

Proteins

Build tissues and serve as enzymes, carriers, receptors and antibodies.

Enzymes

Accelerate reactions and may serve as useful laboratory markers.

DNA & RNA

Carry and use genetic information.

Buffers and pH

Regulation resists harmful changes in acidity and alkalinity.

Glucose metabolism

Energy production, glycogen and blood glucose regulation.

Lipoproteins

LDL, HDL and related particles transport lipids.

Vitamins & minerals

Small quantities can have large physiological effects.

Example

A glucose result

Understanding regulation gives the number meaning beyond memorising a reference range.

Example

Raised enzyme levels

You do not diagnose from one result, but you understand why enzymes can be useful indicators.

This is where knowledge begins to become performance. The goal is not only to copy steps, but to understand why each step is done and how it protects the patient and nurse.

Universal precautions

Use protection for possible exposure.

Hand hygiene

Break the chain of microorganism transmission.

PPE & gloves

Select, don and remove protective equipment safely.

Documentation

Accurate records support continuity of care.

Positioning & body mechanics

Protect patient comfort and staff safety.

Vital signs

Measurements reflect body function and must be obtained accurately.

Skills introduced

Handwashing

When, how and why all hand surfaces are cleaned.

Gloves & PPE

Clean vs sterile gloves; safe donning and doffing.

Patient positioning

Supine, prone, lateral, Fowler's and other positions.

Range of motion

Joint movement according to patient condition.

Hygiene & comfort

Dignity, privacy, skin care and safe movement.

Bed making

Open, closed and occupied beds.

Temperature, pulse, respiration

Technique and factors that affect results.

Blood pressure

Cuff size, arm position and technique.

Example

A surprisingly high blood pressure reading

Check cuff size, position, rest and technique before interpreting the number.

Example

Used sharps after a procedure

Safety continues after the procedure; disposal is part of preventing injury and exposure.
